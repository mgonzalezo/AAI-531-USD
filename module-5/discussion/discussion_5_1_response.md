# Discussion 5.1: The Ethical Challenges of Autonomous Lethal Weapons

## Response

The 2026 geopolitical landscape makes one thing clear: autonomous lethal weapons systems (LAWS) are no longer theoretical—they are active instruments of statecraft. The ongoing U.S.-Iran tensions have demonstrated autonomous surveillance drones and precision-strike systems operating without continuous human intervention. My concern is not whether these weapons are technically impressive; they are. My concern is whether they are actually solving the problems they claim to solve, or whether they are primarily instruments of power projection and coercion—creating deterrence theater while eroding strategic stability for real threats.

Autonomous weapons systems raise deep ethical problems on multiple fronts. Their inability to reliably distinguish combatants from civilians or recognize acts of surrender violates foundational principles of international humanitarian law (Future of Life Institute, 2024). Beyond compliance failures, their decision cycles operate far faster than any human can meaningfully review, removing accountability from the loop. When a civilian is killed by an autonomous system, who bears responsibility—the engineer, the manufacturer, the commanding officer, or the policymaker who approved deployment? The answer is deliberately ambiguous, and that ambiguity is a feature, not a bug, for governments deploying these systems.

Transparency is perhaps the most consequential and unresolved dimension. No international body currently mandates that states disclose the capabilities, limitations, or failure modes of their LAWS. The UN Group of Governmental Experts has debated legally binding instruments on autonomous weapons since 2014 with no binding outcome (United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs, 2023). I have little optimism this will change—strategic advantage depends on opacity. However, this opacity carries real costs: miscalculation, escalation, and civilian harm are all amplified when adversaries cannot accurately model each other's systems. Governments should bear accountability for the strategic instability their secrecy creates, even if there is no legal mechanism to enforce it.

Sharing capability information with adversaries is a genuine dilemma. Transparency could reduce miscalculation and accidental escalation—both catastrophic risks when LAWS operate faster than human de-escalation can respond. However, full disclosure undermines deterrence and introduces exploitable vulnerabilities. A middle path, such as sharing high-level behavioral parameters and failure boundaries through neutral multilateral channels, may be more realistic than full transparency or complete secrecy.

Swarm intelligence compounds the accountability problem substantially. Decentralized, emergent decision-making means no single algorithmic decision can be traced to a responsible agent. As Viveros Alvarez (2024) argues in her UN-affiliated analysis of drone swarms as potential weapons of mass destruction, the collective behavior of swarms can exceed the intent or prediction of their designers. This makes post-hoc attribution of harm nearly impossible and legal accountability frameworks inadequate by design.

LAWS represent a dangerous acceleration of conflict decision-making beyond human response rates—and beyond human moral frameworks. The question is not whether we can build them. The question is whether we should deploy them in the absence of explainability, meaningful oversight, and enforceable accountability.
