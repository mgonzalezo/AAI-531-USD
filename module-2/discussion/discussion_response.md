# Discussion 2.1: Freedom, Autonomy, and Human Dignity - AI Decision-Making for Autonomous Vehicles

## Response

### The Problem with the Trolley Problem Framework

While the Trolley Problem has become a dominant framework for discussing autonomous vehicle (AV) ethics, this framing may actually obscure rather than illuminate the real ethical challenges we face. As Roff (2018) argues in "The Folly of Trolleys," the focus on this thought experiment "detracts from understanding about how autonomous cars actually work, how they 'think,' how much influence humans have over their decision-making processes, and the real ethical issues" (p. 2). The Trolley Problem presents a single-shot decision with complete knowledge and certainty about outcomes—a scenario that bears little resemblance to how autonomous vehicles actually operate.

### How AI Systems Actually Make Decisions

To address whether I should pull the lever in the classic trolley scenario, I must first acknowledge my personal inclination toward inaction—choosing not to intervene and allowing events to unfold as they would naturally. However, this perspective reveals an important asymmetry: it distinguishes between acts of commission and omission, a distinction that may not exist for AI systems.

Autonomous vehicles do not make decisions the way humans do in trolley scenarios. Instead, they operate using Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) that make "sequential decisions in a dynamic environment under conditions of uncertainty" (Roff, 2018, p. 4). The AI is not making a single choice with complete knowledge; rather, it is "choosing a probability of some act changing a state of affairs" based on incomplete sensor data, environmental uncertainty, and learned patterns from thousands of training episodes (Roff, 2018, p. 6). There is no guarantee that something completely novel and unforeseen will not confuse an AV or cause it to act in unforeseeable ways.

### Should AI Prioritize the Greater Good or Individual Rights?

This question presents a false dichotomy when applied to autonomous vehicles. As Roff (2018) explains, "the entire set up of a moral dilemma for a non-moral agent to 'choose' the best action is a false choice" (p. 8). AI systems are not moral agents capable of weighing philosophical principles; they are learning systems optimizing value functions defined by human engineers.

The more relevant question is: **What values should we, as a society, embed into these systems?** The moral choice is not made by the AI in the moment of crisis—it is made by the engineers, policymakers, and society when we decide what to optimize for. Should autonomous vehicles prioritize passenger safety over pedestrian safety? This is not a question the AI answers; it is a question we answer through the value functions and reward signals we design.

Furthermore, focusing on rare trolley-like scenarios distracts from more pressing ethical questions. The real moral landscape involves "second and third-order effects" such as how AVs will reshape urban planning, exacerbate or reduce inequality, impact access to transportation in food deserts, and fundamentally alter "the rights, lives, and benefits of all those around it" (Roff, 2018, p. 15). As Roff notes, autonomous systems are already changing workplaces, streets, and schools, and "we need to ensure that those changes are beneficial, before they are built further into the infrastructure of everyday life" (Crawford & Calo, 2016, as cited in Roff, 2018, p. 10).

### Universal Principles vs. Context-Dependent Ethics

The question of universal ethical principles for AI assumes that such principles can be encoded and applied consistently. However, autonomous vehicles operate in a world of probability distributions, not moral certainties. Even if we identified universal principles—such as preserving human life—translating these into mathematical value functions requires countless context-dependent judgments.

Rather than seeking universal algorithms for trolley-like dilemmas, we should focus on:

1. **Transparency in value functions**: Making explicit what objectives and priorities are embedded in AV systems
2. **Accountability structures**: Ensuring clear lines of responsibility for system behaviors
3. **Inclusive design processes**: Engaging diverse stakeholders in determining what values AVs should optimize
4. **Ongoing evaluation**: Monitoring the societal impacts of AV deployment and adjusting course as needed

As Roff (2018) emphasizes, we must "identify the values that we want to actualize through the engineering, design and deployment of technologies, like self-driving cars" (p. 11). This is fundamentally a normative, political choice about what kind of society we want to build—not a mathematical optimization problem.

### Conclusion

The Trolley Problem, while intellectually provocative, offers limited guidance for the ethics of autonomous vehicles. The real challenge is not programming cars to make heroic split-second decisions, but rather ensuring that the values embedded in their learning systems, the infrastructure built around them, and the societal changes they enable align with human dignity, freedom, and equity. As Roff (2018) concludes, "the application of Trolleyology to this problem is not only a distraction, it is a fallacy" (p. 13). We must shift our focus from hypothetical dilemmas to the concrete value choices and systemic impacts that will shape how autonomous vehicles affect all members of society.

My initial preference for inaction in the trolley scenario reflects a human moral intuition about the difference between causing harm and allowing it to occur. However, AI systems operate without such intuitions—they simply execute the objectives we program into them. This places the moral responsibility squarely on us: not to solve abstract trolley problems, but to carefully deliberate about what we want these powerful technologies to optimize for and how we want them to reshape our world.
